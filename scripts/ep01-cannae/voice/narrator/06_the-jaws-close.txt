The battle starts. The Roman block charges into Hannibal's center, and at first, it's working. The Gauls and Spaniards are pushed back. Step by step. The crescent flattens. Then it caves inward.

The Romans are winning. They can feel it. They push deeper and deeper into the gap. More men crowd in behind them.

And then Hannibal says one word: 'Now.'

On both sides, the African soldiers turn inward and slam into the Roman flanks with their spears. At the same time, Hannibal's heavy cavalry have already crushed the Roman horsemen by the river. But they don't stop to celebrate. They don't chase the runners.

They ride all the way around, behind the Roman army. They help the Numidians drive off the last Roman cavalry units. Then they charge straight into the Roman rear.

The Roman army is completely surrounded. So tightly packed they can barely raise their swords. The men in the front don't know what's happening. The men in the back can't escape. They're crushed together like sardines in a can.

By sunset, it was over. Polybius says around seventy thousand Romans died that day. Other historians say forty-eight thousand. Either way, it was one of the bloodiest single days of battle in all of history.

The consul Paullus died on the field. Varro escaped — running away on horseback, his overconfidence now a liability. Hannibal lost somewhere between six and eight thousand men.
