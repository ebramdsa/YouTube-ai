# Assyria: The Most Feared Empire on Earth, Gone in a Few Years
## Full Script — Narrator Only (~13 minutes)

**Language:** English
**Voice:** One narrator, same as episode 2 (Gemini TTS, Algenib): dramatic, dry humour, brisk
**Characters:** Silent, animate only
**Lessons applied (reports/2026-10-14-cannae-analysis.md):** the shock and the episode's question land in the first 10 seconds; every chapter ends on a line that pulls into the next one.

---

## 0. COLD OPEN (0:00–0:40)

[*Night. A huge walled city on a river, on fire. Silhouettes of soldiers pouring through a broken gate.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"612 BC. The biggest city on Earth is burning."

"Its name is Nineveh. Capital of Assyria. The most feared empire the world had ever seen."

[*Map: a red empire stretching from Egypt to Iran.*]

"Forty years earlier, Assyria ruled from Egypt to Iran. Kings sent it gold just to be left alone."

[*The red empire shrinks to a dot and disappears.*]

"A few years from now, it won't exist at all. Not a city. Not a king. Not even a name most people remember."

"So how does the scariest empire on the planet just... vanish?"

[*TITLE CARD: ASSYRIA*]

---

## 1. THE MOST FEARED EMPIRE (0:40–2:10)

[*Map of the Middle East. Assyria a small red patch on the Tigris, in what is now northern Iraq.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"Assyria started small. A handful of cities on the Tigris River, in what is now northern Iraq."

[*The red patch spreads in pulses.*]

"But over about three hundred years, its kings turned it into a war machine. Every spring, the army marched out. Every autumn, it came back richer."

[*Assyrian soldiers in pointed helmets, with iron swords, archers, and a battering ram with a little tower.*]

"They had iron weapons, professional soldiers, cavalry, and the best siege engineers in the world. Battering rams on wheels. Siege towers. Tunnels under city walls. If you hid behind your walls, the Assyrians just took the walls apart."

[*Map: red covers Mesopotamia, Syria, the Levant, and finally Egypt.*]

"By the six hundred and seventies BC, Assyria had conquered Babylon, the cities of the Levant, and even Egypt. It was the largest empire the world had seen so far."

[*Nineveh by day: huge walls, gates, palaces, gardens, canals.*]

"And at its centre sat Nineveh. Twelve kilometres of walls. Fifteen gates. Palaces covered in carved stone, gardens watered by canals and aqueducts. In its day, quite possibly the largest city on Earth."

"There was just one thing Assyria never had. Friends."

---

## 2. HOW TO TERRIFY THE WORLD (2:10–3:40)

[*Palace corridor lined with carved stone reliefs; a nervous foreign envoy walks through.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"Assyrian kings ruled with one simple idea. Fear."

"Their palace walls were covered with carved scenes of what happened to people who rebelled. Cities burning. Prisoners marched away in chains. Enemies punished in ways we're not going to show you on this channel."

[*The envoy, sweating, reaching the throne room.*]

"Foreign ambassadors had to walk past those pictures on their way to meet the king. That was the point. The walls were the message."

[*Long lines of families with carts, walking across a map from one corner to another.*]

"And the Assyrians had another trick. Deportation. When a region rebelled, they didn't just punish it. They moved its people hundreds of kilometres away, and brought strangers in to replace them. Over three centuries, historians estimate that millions of people were uprooted this way."

[*Split screen: a happy Assyrian king on one side, a crowd of angry faces from every corner of the map on the other.*]

"It worked. For a long time, nobody dared to fight back. But think about what it does to your neighbours when your whole foreign policy is terror."

"Everyone obeyed Assyria. And everyone was waiting."

---

## 3. THE LAST GREAT KING (3:40–5:00)

[*A king with a long curled beard and a tall hat, reading a clay tablet. Then, the same king spearing a lion from a chariot.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"The last great king of Assyria was Ashurbanipal. He was a strange mix. On one hand, a scholar, who boasted that he could read old texts nobody else could. He built one of the first great libraries in history at Nineveh, with tens of thousands of clay tablets."

"On the other hand, he liked to be shown killing lions with his bare hands. Mostly with a spear. Occasionally, according to his artists, by grabbing them by the tail."

[*Two brothers on two thrones, Nineveh and Babylon, glaring at each other.*]

"And he had a family problem. His father had split the empire: Ashurbanipal in Nineveh, and his older brother, Shamash-shum-ukin, on the throne of Babylon. That went about as well as you'd expect. The brothers ended up at war. Ashurbanipal besieged Babylon for years, until the city starved and his brother died in a fire."

[*Ashurbanipal on the throne, older, tired. The clay tablets around him go blank.*]

"Assyria won. Again. But then something odd happens. In the last years of Ashurbanipal's reign, the records go quiet. We're not even completely sure when he died. Probably around 631 BC."

"And when he did, the war machine had nobody left who knew how to drive it."

---

## 4. BROTHERS AND THRONES (5:00–6:20)

[*An empty throne. Several crowned figures reach for it at once.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"After Ashurbanipal came a mess. His sons fought each other for the throne. Generals backed one side or the other. In a few short years, Assyria had more kings than it had peace."

[*Babylon's walls. A new figure steps up on them, raising a fist: Nabopolassar.*]

"And down south, Babylon was watching. In 626 BC, a Babylonian leader named Nabopolassar declared himself king of Babylon. Not Assyria's governor. King."

[*Assyrian armies marching south, getting stuck, marching back.*]

"The Assyrians did what they always did. They sent the army. But this time, it didn't work. For ten years they fought over Babylonia, and they could never quite finish the job."

[*Map: the red empire with cracks spreading across it.*]

"Meanwhile, the rest of the empire noticed. Egypt slipped away. The kingdoms of the Levant stopped paying. The great machine was running out of fuel."

"And in the mountains to the east, someone else was getting ready."

---

## 5. THE ENEMIES UNITE (6:20–7:40)

[*Zagros mountains. Horsemen with bows, in a different style from the Assyrians: the Medes.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"The Medes. A people from the mountains of what is now Iran. For years they had been raided and bullied by Assyria. Now they had a strong king, Cyaxares, and an army of skilled horse archers."

[*Map: blue arrow from the east hits the city of Assur. Flames.*]

"In 614 BC, the Medes struck. Not at Nineveh, but at Assur, the oldest city of Assyria, home of its national god. They took it, and sacked it."

[*Nabopolassar arrives at the smoking ruins; Cyaxares is already there. The two shake hands.*]

"The Babylonian army arrived too late to join in. But according to the Babylonian records, the two kings met at the ruins of Assur, and made an alliance. Later tradition says it was sealed with a royal marriage, too."

[*Two arrows, blue from the east and gold from the south, both pointing at Nineveh.*]

"For centuries, Assyria's enemies had been divided. That was how one empire could rule them all. Now the two strongest of them were on the same side. And they had a target."

"Nineveh."

---

## 6. THE FALL OF NINEVEH (7:40–9:30)

[*Nineveh's walls. Two huge armies camped around them.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"In the summer of 612 BC, the Medes and the Babylonians laid siege to the biggest city in the world."

"Nineveh had been built to be untouchable. Twelve kilometres of walls. Fifteen gates. But a wall that long needs a lot of men to defend it. And after twenty years of civil war and rebellion, Assyria didn't have them anymore."

[*Calendar pages flip: three months.*]

"According to the Babylonian Chronicle, the siege lasted about three months. Then the walls were broken, and the city fell."

[*Smoke over the palace. The carved walls cracking in the heat.*]

"The attackers looted the palaces, and burned them. The king of Assyria, Sin-sharra-ishkun, died as his capital fell."

[*Text card: LEGEND.*]

"Later Greek writers told a more dramatic story. A flooded river smashed through the walls, and the last king burned himself in his palace with all his treasure. Historians treat those stories carefully. But archaeologists did find something real."

[*Archaeologists brushing dirt away at a narrow gate.*]

"At one of Nineveh's gates, they found that the defenders had tried to block it, making the entrance narrower and narrower. And they found the remains of people who died fighting right there, in the gateway."

[*Burned clay tablets in the ashes of the library.*]

"And here's a twist. The fire that destroyed the palaces baked Ashurbanipal's clay tablets hard. Which is a big reason so many of them survived. Assyria's enemies tried to wipe it out, and accidentally preserved its library."

"But Assyria wasn't quite finished. Not yet."

---

## 7. THE LAST STAND AT HARRAN (9:30–10:50)

[*A young Assyrian prince crowning himself in a small city in the west: Harran.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"A member of the royal family escaped west, to the city of Harran, and crowned himself king. Ashur-uballit the Second. The last king of Assyria. His kingdom was now basically one city and whatever soldiers he could find."

[*An Egyptian pharaoh on a chariot, marching north: Necho II.*]

"And he found help in a surprising place. Egypt. The Egyptians had once been conquered by Assyria. But a weak Assyria in the north looked a lot better to them than a strong Babylon. So Pharaoh Necho the Second marched north to help."

[*Map: Harran falls to blue and gold. Then an Egyptian-Assyrian counter-attack bounces off.*]

"It wasn't enough. In 610 BC, the Babylonians and the Medes took Harran. The next year, Ashur-uballit and his Egyptian allies tried to take it back, and failed."

[*Ashur-uballit walks off the edge of the map. A question mark.*]

"And then, he simply disappears from history. No record of his death. No last battle. Just... nothing."

"After almost fifteen hundred years, Assyria was gone. From its peak to nothing in about a generation, and its heartland lost in just a few years."

---

## 8. WHY DID IT FALL? (10:50–12:10)

[*A chalkboard titled "WHY?". The narrator's pointer taps each reason as it appears.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"So how does the most feared empire on Earth collapse that fast? Historians point to a few things working together."

[*Reason 1: two crowned figures pulling a crown apart.*]

"One. Civil war. Assyria spent its last decades fighting itself. Brother against brother, son against son. Every war at home was an army that couldn't defend the borders."

[*Reason 2: a tiny Assyrian soldier trying to hold up a giant map.*]

"Two. It was too big. An empire held together by the army only works while the army can be everywhere. Once it couldn't, the whole thing came apart at once."

[*Reason 3: the crowd of angry faces from episode section 2 returns.*]

"Three. Everybody hated them. Fear keeps people quiet, but it doesn't make anyone loyal. When Assyria stumbled, nobody helped it up. They lined up to kick it."

[*Reason 4: cracked dry fields, a small question mark next to it.*]

"And four, maybe, the weather. Some recent studies of ancient climate suggest the region went through serious droughts in the seventh century BC, which would have hit an empire that fed huge cities very hard. That one's still debated. But it might have made everything else worse."

"No single blow killed Assyria. It was a lot of cracks, and then one very hard push."

---

## 9. OUTRO (12:10–13:00)

[*Grass-covered mounds by the Tigris. A Greek soldier with a crested helmet walks past, looking puzzled.*]

**NARRATOR:**
"Here's how completely Assyria vanished. About two hundred years after Nineveh fell, a Greek army marched right past its ruins. Their leader, Xenophon, wrote about the huge abandoned walls. He didn't even know whose city it had been."

[*Nineteenth-century explorers digging; a giant winged bull with a human head rising out of the dirt.*]

"It took until the eighteen-forties for archaeologists to dig Nineveh up again, and find the palaces, the carvings, and Ashurbanipal's library."

[*Modern map. A small group of people under a flag.*]

"The empire is gone. But the Assyrian people aren't. Their descendants still live today, in Iraq, Syria, and all over the world, and they still call themselves Assyrians."

"The empire that ruled by fear disappeared. The people survived it."

[*Title card: Next episode — the Nabataeans*]

"Next time: the Nabataeans. Desert traders who carved an entire city into the rock. Subscribe to Spears and Tears, so you don't miss it."

## PRODUCTION NOTES

**Characters needed:** Ashurbanipal (long curled beard, tall fez-like royal hat), his brother Shamash-shum-ukin, Assyrian soldiers (pointed helmets, iron swords, archers), Assyrian siege engine (battering ram with tower), a nervous foreign envoy, Nabopolassar (Babylonian king), Cyaxares and Median horse archers, Sin-sharra-ishkun (last king of Nineveh), Ashur-uballit II, Pharaoh Necho II (reuse the Egyptian kit from episode 2), Xenophon (Greek hoplite), 19th-century archaeologist, lamassu (winged bull). Reuse from episode 2: Egyptian soldiers and chariot, map base, clay tablets, carved walls (stone filter), historian.

**Map work:** extend the episode 2 Middle East map east to the Zagros and Iran (Nineveh, Assur, Babylon, Harran, Media), keep the same palette.

**Sources:** Babylonian Chronicles (Fall of Nineveh Chronicle, ABC 3); Assyrian royal inscriptions and palace reliefs (British Museum); Eckart Frahm, *Assyria: The Rise and Fall of the World's First Empire* (2023); Karen Radner, *Ancient Assyria: A Very Short Introduction*; Bustenay Oded, *Mass Deportations and Deportees in the Neo-Assyrian Empire*; David Stronach's excavations at the Halzi Gate, Nineveh; Sinha et al. 2019 (Science Advances) on 7th-century BC drought; Xenophon, *Anabasis* 3.4.

**Accuracy notes:**
- Dates follow the standard chronology: Nabopolassar king of Babylon 626; Assur falls to the Medes 614; Nineveh falls 612 after a roughly three-month siege (Babylonian Chronicle); Harran falls 610; failed attempt to retake it 609.
- Ashurbanipal's death date is uncertain (c. 631, some argue 627); the script says "probably around 631".
- Deportation figures are scholarly estimates (Oded: about 4.5 million over three centuries); the script says "millions" and "historians estimate".
- The Medes–Babylonian marriage (Amytis) comes from later tradition (Berossus), flagged as such. The flood and self-immolation stories come from later Greek writers (Ctesias via Diodorus), flagged as legend.
- Halzi Gate: narrowed by the defenders; skeletons of people killed in the gateway (Stronach, 1989–90).
- Drought: recent and debated (Sinha et al. 2019), flagged as "maybe" and "still debated".
- Xenophon (Anabasis 3.4) passed the ruins of Nimrud and Nineveh (he calls them Larisa and Mespila) without naming Assyria.
- Violence is described, never shown; keep reliefs stylised and off-screen per the channel's tone.
